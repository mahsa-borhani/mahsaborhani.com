A team of AI agents, with brakes

22 releases

I built a production AI agent system myself, in Claude Code: about twenty roles across sales, operations, reporting, advertising and QA, working every day against live store, advertising, search and CRM data. Twenty-two versioned releases between April and October 2026.

The business problem

The business needed monitoring, reporting, forecasting and listings work done every day without adding headcount, and safely: nothing published, spent or deleted by an AI on its own.

How it’s built

  • Each agent has one role and a written playbook. Work moves through a pipeline: drafted from live data, checked by a second agent, approved by a person.
  • Monitoring, anomaly alerts, reporting, forecasting and documentation run daily; listings, titles and product-feed fixes run through the same pipeline.
  • Four rules, enforced in code rather than remembered: the agent that produces never reviews; anything customer-facing needs two independent sources; anything irreversible waits for a person; every change is versioned in Git.
  • Least privilege: its Microsoft 365 access is an Entra ID app holding a single Sites.Selected permission that reaches only one SharePoint site, approved by IT from a ready-to-approve request I wrote. One of about 15 Microsoft 365 requests I’ve worked through with IT.
  • Forecasts checked against outcomes: the model resamples our real orders, out-predicted the target three 30-day sprints running, and I wrote the fourth sprint’s range down before any order existed.

Why the rules exist

In August I reviewed 32 incidents and found one cause behind them: the agent that did the work had also certified it. I rebuilt the system around separation of duties. Now QA sends back four in ten first drafts before anyone sees them, and half of content drafts.

Rigor, in numbers

About 363 pull requests (327 merged) and 996 tickets between April and November 2026; 26 recurring scheduled tasks; and 94 lessons, each written as a rule beside the mistake that taught it.

QA’s most common catches are a wrong fact or number (about 57% of returns) and an unsupported claim (about 40%). When I found that the done-checker passed any ticket that merely named an existing file, and 189 tickets had passed that way, I rewrote it. Once a brief exists, work goes live the same day to within a week; the fastest, four technical pages, went from first review to live in one working day.

The brakes, in practice

An automated finding once proposed cancelling five container orders as duplicates. The human-approval rule held; I re-derived the truth from purchase orders, internal documents and shipping records, and all five were real.

People and adoption

I packaged the system into a kit with a plain-English guide, role playbooks and safety rules, wrote the AI adoption plan for leadership and an AI-assisted interview kit for hiring, and coached six people one-on-one on AI tools, from the business owner and controller to the supply chain manager and sales lead.

I’m now coordinating the AI implementation across two companies in the group, on a platform I brought in.

What I learned

A gate that never fails anything is decoration.
